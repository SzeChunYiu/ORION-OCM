# Causal inference requires intervention semantics

Status: finite structural-model derivations, with an explicit observational
nonidentification counterexample. No neural computation is needed.

## CAU1: a causal specialization of the process primitive

Take a finite acyclic structural model
\(X_i=f_i(X_{\mathrm{pa}(i)},U_i)\), with a specified joint law of the
exogenous variables \(U\). A topological ordering recursively defines the
joint observational law. An intervention \(do(X_A=x_A)\) replaces those
structural equations by constants, leaving the remaining functions and the
law of \(U\) unchanged. This replacement semantics is an additional causal
assumption within \(\mathsf P\), not a consequence of correlation.

When the exogenous variables are mutually independent and the induced local
kernels are \(p_i(x_i\mid x_{\mathrm{pa}(i)})\), the intervention law is
\[
p^{do(x_A)}(x)=1_{\{X_A=x_A\}}
\prod_{i\notin A}p_i(x_i\mid x_{\mathrm{pa}(i)}).
\]
**Proof.** Generate variables in topological order. An intervened node has its
fixed point-mass law. Every other node draws from its unchanged exogenous source,
independent of earlier sources, hence its original conditional kernel.
Multiplication of these conditional laws gives the formula. Shared hidden
causes invalidate this observed-variable factorization unless included in the
joint model. Null observational parent contexts need specified mechanisms;
observational data alone need not identify their intervention behavior.

## CAU2: observational equivalence does not determine actions

Let \(U\) be a fair bit. In model A, \(X=U,Y=X\); in model B,
\(X=U,Y=U\). Both have observational distribution
\(\Pr(X=Y=0)=\Pr(X=Y=1)=1/2\). In A,
\(\Pr(Y=1\mid do(X=1))=1\); in B it is \(1/2\).

**Proof of nonidentification.** Every finite or infinite observational sample
has the same distribution in both models. Any data-only procedure therefore
has the same output law in both. It cannot identify two different intervention
values with certainty. A controlled randomized intervention on \(X\), or a
substantive restriction excluding one model, distinguishes them. Additional
observational precision cannot repair this missing premise.

## CAU3: identified average effect under explicit assumptions

Let potential outcome \(Y(a)\) be defined by the intervention model. Assume
consistency \(Y=Y(A)\), conditional exchangeability
\(Y(a)\perp A\mid Z\), and positivity
\(\Pr(A=a\mid Z=z)>0\) for every target stratum with \(p(z)>0\).
Then
\[
\mathbb E[Y(a)]
=\sum_zp(z)\mathbb E[Y\mid A=a,Z=z].
\]
**Proof.** Total expectation gives
\(\sum_zp(z)\mathbb E[Y(a)\mid Z=z]\).
Exchangeability permits conditioning additionally on \(A=a\); positivity
makes that observed conditional estimable; consistency replaces \(Y(a)\) by
\(Y\). These are separate assumptions. Selecting \(Z\) merely because it
correlates with treatment/outcome need not establish exchangeability.

For a sequential finite-horizon policy, use history \(H_t\) and propensities
\(g_t(a\mid H_t)>0\) wherever target policy \(\pi_t\) has support. Under
sequential exchangeability and consistency,
\[
\mathbb E_g\left[R\prod_{t=0}^{H-1}
\frac{\pi_t(A_t\mid H_t)}{g_t(A_t\mid H_t)}\right]
=\mathbb E_\pi R.
\]
**Proof.** Expand the finite trajectory expectation as a sum of initial law,
action propensities, controlled observation kernels and terminal reward.
Multiplying the ratio cancels each \(g_t\) and replaces it by \(\pi_t\),
which is precisely the target-policy trajectory law. Bounded rewards and finite
spaces make the sum legitimate. Rare propensities can cause large variance;
an adaptive estimate still needs a valid bound, not just this expectation
identity. Without exchangeability, observed conditionals are not necessarily
the kernels generated by policy intervention.

## CAU4: acquisition ablation and mechanism claims

To test whether retained knowledge \(k\) causes a benefit, define interventions
that replace \(k\) while holding the declared initial context, randomization
scheme, interface and task law fixed. Compare complete distributions of loss
and resource traces under those interventions. Paired seeds are a variance
reduction device, not by themselves an identification theorem.

An ablation can change computation cost, accessible information or other state.
Then it identifies the effect of that entire intervention. A claim about one
specific mediator additionally needs a justified intervention isolating that
mediator. Versioned evidence and an exact source fingerprint do not establish
that a retained method was used causally. This retains the repository's
distinction between an imported fact, a verbalized proof and an acquired method
that changes future computation.
